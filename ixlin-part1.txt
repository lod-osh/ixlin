A pearly sky hung over the plains, grumbling at the rabbits who scrambled across the marsh. They stopped to nibble the berries that had fallen from the foxfruit bush before scurrying through the long spirals of rusty barbed wire that set the boundary of some long-forgotten property. 

In the center of the vast, treeless stretch, an abandoned chapel to the Unadorned sank into the black earth. Its once white brick was stained with long black strands of filth. A horde of young frogs jumped from the murky water and attached themselves to the slimy walls, singing a raspy harmony with the thunder. 

The submerged east corner of the chapel was overtaken by thorny heaps of ivy. Alongside deadly thorns bloomed the mourning flower, whose sticky sweet scent wafted over the marsh. 

Clinging to the bent weather vane atop the chapel's cupola was a bright and dutiful tapestry spider named Tychus.

From this vantage point, he could see all the way to the edge of the marsh. Mud elk walked the perimeter where fish got trapped in shallow waters, and were easily skewered by the sword-like bone protrusions from their skulls. They hunted in pairs, each allowing the other to eat the fish that wriggled on the end of his antlers. Broad hooves prevented them from sinking into the mud, which was essential for the quick retreats from Kladramara, the giant long-snouted, blind crocodile who was as old as the marsh itself.

Tychus scanned the muddy waters and finally spotted her, looking thinner than ever. Her once lustrous scales were dull and cracked. Her yellow eyes betrayed a hungry desperation. Hunting had become more difficult as she grew slower each year, and even the young and sickly elk starting evading her bite. They became less fearful by the day, wandering a little further into Kladramara's domain. 

That worried Tychus, not only because he hated to see his friend suffer in her old age, but also because in greater numbers the mud elk would overfish the waters, they would devour the vegetation, they would stomp on frogs and rabbits and worst of all, they would bring the wolves again.

A crackling vein of lightning slashed the sky open and bled rain onto the marsh. Having saved his least favorite part of the assessment until last, Tychus steeled himself and entered the little belltower, then lowered himself on a thin black line into the flooded interior of the chapel. 

Black water lapped against decaying pews. Tychus dropped onto the neck of a headless statue standing in an alcove, ran to the finger of its outstretched hand, and jumped over the ghoulfish swimming around the statue's ankles. He landed on the top edge of a pew in the last row and ran along it to the chapel's center, then hopped from pew to pew toward the front of the sanctuary. 

Ghoulfish rose to the surface along the tops of the pews, inhaling Tychus' scent as he ran by. He scanned the water as he ran, spotting the floating limbs of at least three rotfairies who had been snatched out of the air, drowned, nibbled upon, and dismembered, though not necessarily in that order. 

In retaliation, the rotfairies had left writhing ghoulfish impaled on tiny spears around the edge of the altar. In the center of the altar was a ghastly pile of mud nests, riddled with holes and crowned with twisted spires that nearly reached the ceiling. 

Tychus jumped to the altar, his glittering eyes scanning the rotfairy citadel. Though his venom could wither them in seconds, the fairies were wasp-minded and malicious, incapable of either empathy or forethought. They used their humanoid hands to craft and wield weapons, which put Tychus at a disadvantage. And though Tychus was ashamed to admit it even to himself, the awful faces they had evolved over the ages to both mimick and mock humanity both disgusted and terrified him. 

Tychus stepped softly into the semicircle of nests before him, counting the holes that housed one or more working fairies and the pupae who had been assigned to them. Fortunately, no fairies seemed to be present at the moment, but he shuddered as his passing disturbed the sleep in the broodhouse and dozens of larvae stuck together in one congealed mass cried out to him for food and attention. 

Rotfairies were notoriously negligent toward their young, but knowing this didn't slow Tychus down as he rushed past the storehouse and eyeballed a three-week supply of rothoney. The pollen of the mourning flower already smelled unpleasant, but the final product emitted a rancid stench.

The fairies were never far from the pollen of the mourning flower. Its abundance allowed for lots of free time, which the rotfairies usually spent dueling or capturing and torturing lizards and insects. The tiny bones and remnants of exoskeletons told Tychus their sadistic habits had not changed. 

He breathed a sigh of relief at the realization that he would not have to meet any rotfairies today, jumped onto the east wall, and scurried over a broken windowpane back into the rain, under the cover of the ivy. 

Launching himself from thistle to weed, he landed finally on the edge of the end of a dirt road that was quickly turning to mud. He ascended a tilting pole thirty feet to the top and stepped onto the black cable that stretched to the next pole, barely visible in the misty distance.

Tychus gripped the cable tightly, waiting patiently for the rain to stop hitting the line. 

He scanned the rolling hills that rose above the fog to the east, now marred with black squares of scorched earth. Humble farmhouses had been smashed to splinters by knights on a fool's errand for a holy trinket of some sort. 

To the west was the source of the destruction - a city in the throes of madness, to Tychus' estimation. 

And straight ahead along the line on which he sat was the road north. It led all the way to Ixlin, the witch township for which he worked.

The sky closed up and the rain softened. Tychus, having mentally prepared his report, gripped the cable with his two rear legs, tapping out a sort of Morse code with the other six, each with a different pattern of dots and dashes.

The vibrations Tychus tapped out on the cable alarmed a lazy bangbeetle resting in the slack of the line; he jolted awake and ignited the volatile contents in his belly, exploding like a cannon. The echo shot through the tall grass, scrambling the rabbits and crows into an annoyed confusion. 

Tychus, being a consummate professional, did not react; he simply added the occurrence to his report. His legs continued tapping out their polyrhythmic message with precision and enthusiasm without hesitation.

The coded pulses rode from pole to pole until they descended the first branching wire to a stone cottage nestled in foxfruit bushes.

Three coats of black paint covered the cottage's few windows. Inside, the soft light from shaded lamps illuminated a cozy parlor. The forest green walls were cluttered with paintings in frames of various sizes, shapes, and materials, with none of them matching. 

The ceiling was covered with a network of paper lanterns and strings of purple glass beads that tinkled softly from the subtle breeze slipping through the front door, which had been propped open by the weathered head of a statue.

A solitary ember burned from the ashes in the fireplace. A white long-haired cat slept in front of it, refusing to accept that the fire had gone out. 

The message crawled the line from ceiling to floor, into a black rotary phone that stood on its little table with eight legs, much like Tychus'.

The telephone jangled enthusiastically until six year-old Ylla lifted its receiver. She raised it with both hands to her ear and stared blankly at the wall, absorbing the patterns of six distinct tones warbling dots and dashes until the message had ended.

Ylla had developed a recent interest in the intricacies of phone etiquette and safety ever since her friend Pyna's mother was infected with a flatterworm. As was the parasite's habit, it entered the host's ear directly from the line, usually during a long conversation. It sat in her inner ear and whispered sweet nothings. Pyna said her mother would blush and say thank you while doing the dishes, and only went to see the nanomancer to have it removed after her husband insisted. Ylla thought she wouldn't mind having a flatterworm.

She replaced the receiver and ran to the back door. She turned the knob and let it swing open while she held on, pulling her forward until she was leaning over the first of the five steps to the garden on her tiptoes.

Her mother, her older brother Flick, and their helper Balo were working in the garden. Her sister Dyetta, the eldest, was sternly hanging linens on the line.

Radeka Endriver wore her black hair in a loose bun. The wind pulled the strands of silver from her temples. She wiped her soiled gloves on the front of her coveralls.

A few paces from the front door and they were on the road into town. Flick breathed deeply, relieved to be out of his mother's sight. 

Flick took long steps on the road to town, trying to keep up with Balo. Dull yellow grass stretched to the horizon in every direction under a blank white sky. The cool air tasted sweet and metallic.

One of the lines connected the five poles that ascended the bluff on the east side of the town and climbed to the tallest point in Ixlin - the steepled roof of Murkwood Manor, which had been hidden by a thick copse of trees along the ridge, until their recent and sudden removal exposed its blank walls.

The black cable penetrated the manor. It climbed between the walls and stretched across the study and out onto the balcony, pulled taut. It had been tied into a noose, and a leathery skinned old sailor who stood on the railing was tightening it around his neck. 

He hesitated. The long wisps of grey hair that protruded from his temple danced in the wind. He felt the cable around his neck pulse with irregular vibrations. The sailor stood frozen for a moment, fingertips on the line around his throat, interpreting the strange message. 

The old sailor's eyes lit up. He removed the noose and returned to the desk in his study, rifling through its drawers until 
he found a handrawn map.

The face was an amateurishly rendered set of two deep, asymmetrical eye holes and a wide slash beneath them.

The sailor took a few shuffled steps toward the clay golem as if it might suddenly spring to life. He stood on his toes and held his breatn. His shaking fingers tucked the ripped page into the slash until the golem's mouth was filled with instruction. The sailor gave the golem an affectionate pat on its fat clay belly and dashed from the room down the hall, leaving the door open. 

The clay golem stood very still. It was very good at this; it rested in utter serenity knowing that it was clay, and clay never needed to be or do anything else, unless it was properly motivated. But it had been fed fine, buttery paper inscribed with the fine, buttery words of its master. The commands were sweet to the taste as it rolled them over its clay tongue. But as sweet as it was to receive instruction, it was much sweeter to obey them.

"You mean to stab him? I would never do that," said Flick. 

"But you must," she approached him from behind, her candlight shadow climbing the wall. She placed her hand on his shoulder and he tensed.

"You must be clean to enter the Great Garden," she intoned. "In order to do so, you must cast your filth upon he who dies for us! He offers himself as sacrifice. Take the spear and do what we do best, so that he can make your dirty spirit pure!"

"Huh?"

"Ignorant boy! Stay right here!" Her wooden heels clacked away on the stone floor.

The tiny, bloodied head on which Flick's eyes had remained transfixed rose again. This time he spoke.

"She's going upstairs to get the Book of Order," the tiny man said. "You have to get me out of here. This is my only chance."

"How? Your arms are nailed to a tree."

"You'll have to pull me off of it. Make it quick, so they rip right through. Otherwise you'll tear my arms off."

"Maybe I can pull out the nails," Flick hesitated. 

"There's no time! I'll live, boy, I promise! Do it now! I'm begging you! I'm being tortured! I'll give you whatever you want! Gold, women, wine... she's coming back! Do it!"

The sound of the woman's wooden heels drew closer. "I hope that's a filthy confession I'm hearing! Don't forget to draw blood as soon as you've shared your awful secrets."

The man's eyes widened in terror and his bloody bearded chin quivered as he watched her approach. "You'll be a hero from this day forth, boy! Now!"

Flick suppressed every memory of his mother directing him to be as gentle as possible with small, injured things, gripped the man around the middle like a doll and yanked and turned to run in one motion. 

The man screamed. Flick ran for the door, brushed against the woman's frail body, and knocked her to the ground. 

Flick escaped into the sunlight and placed the little man in his shirt pocket. The man's face was twisted with pain. He tore his rags to wrap around his mutilated hands and curled up defensively. A dot of blood stained through Flick's shirt.

"Don't stop! Keep moving!" the little man shouted through his teeth. "Take the next left!" Flick veered left when the avenue forked, and followed the tiny man's directions as he led them off the path into the tall grass, toward a copse of silver oak.

"Wait!" the man said. "This is close enough. Get down low."

Flick obeyed and dropped to his knees in the tall grass.

"Hold me up so I can see," the little man commanded, crawling out of Flick's pocket and into the palm of his hand. He raised his arm so the man could see above the grass.

He froze in place, staring at what looked like a swarm of large insects darting through the distant trees.

Flick glanced up at the man standing in his hand. His bare back was covered in cuts and scars, but none of them were nearly as big or ghastly as the two that ran down each shoulder blade. 

"What are you looking at?"

"My family," the little man replied.

Flick squinted his eyes at the flying creatures and back at the man standing in his palm and released a gasp. 

"You... had wings?"

The man didn't answer. 

"Don't you want to go back to them?"

The man didn't answer. He turned to face Flick and said "I can't. Put me back in your pocket."

Flick obeyed, and took him back to the path. 

"Place me on your open palm, boy. I'd like to face you." Flick complied and the tiny man continued. "I owe you an explanation. But first, thank you for saving me. My name is Zeet."

"I'm Flick. Why don't you want to go back to your family?"

"I am a member of the Seelie Court - a fairy."

"Like a rotfairy?"

"No!" Zeet grimaced. "Nothing like a rotfairy. True fairies are quite clever and beautiful. When we're intact, that is. That bitch caught me in a net one morning while I was gathering berries. Sadistic cunt tore my wings off. My beautiful wings. God damn it. Do you have a smoke?"

Flick shook his head.

Zeet sighed but continued. "We are not like you. We can withstand a great deal, and we heal very quickly. My hands will reassemble themselves. In a week, all these cuts and scars will be gone. But my wings will never grow back. We take great pride in our gossamer wings. When a lady fairy seeks out a mate, she looks for the strongest, most lovely pair of wings. To lose our wings is to lose everything. I could never go back to them. The shame and humiliation would be unbearable."

"But won't they take care of you?"

"Oh, of course! But don't you see, I will always be an invalid to them. I will always be broken, and they will pity me. I am too proud for pity. And I won't be a burden. Except to you, of course," he half-smiled.

"You won't be a burden," said Flick. "You aren't much bigger than Thaddeus."

"Thaddeus?"

Hearing his new name, the oil frog climbed out of Flick's hair and hopped onto his extended arm. 

"Mud chicken!" Zeet screamed, and ran to the end of Flick's middle finger. Thaddeus' tongue shot out and grabbed Zeet's ankle.

"Bad frog!" Flick scolded, snatching him off his arm with the other hand. Warm oil streamed through his fingers.

"I'll not keep company with the mud chicken, boy," Zeet huffed.

"I won't let Thaddeus hurt you. You'll have to get along."

"Where do you live? To the south?"

"Umm..."

"Point your finger at home."

The hand holding the frog raised and pointed southwest. "It's... that way. I think."

"Fine. I won't trouble you much longer. Take me with you halfway home. I'll start a new life in a new grove."

"Oh no!" Flick gasped. "I forgot to stay in the plaza!" He jammed the fairy in his pocket and dashed back toward the plaza, past the Dying Man shop.

Flick's fear of angering Balo eclipsed his fear of the accusatory mob, but the latter had completely dissipated. The tables were cleared of their wares, and the few people still in the plaza were leaving.

As he paused to catch his breath, bitter anxiety rose in Flick's throat. 

"Don't panic," the fairy said. "Who are you looking for?"

"Balo," Flick replied. "He works for my... he's my friend."

Flick's eyes lit up as he remembered something. "Third Alley! That's where he's meeting his stupid friend."

"Ah, I know a shortcut!" said Zeet. "There's a stable behind the alchemist's. Me and the boys used to buzz through that shop and try not to get swatted. The storefront is right down there," he pointed. 

Flick dashed to the alchemist's door, found it ajar, and slipped inside. The door brushed against a series of chimes that played a wistful chord. 

Thaddeus arose from atop Flick's head to look around. Stacks of animal cages lined the walls from floor to ceiling. Small primates with round, protruding black eyes and long, shimmering white fur clung to the bars and sniffed the air as Flick came closer.

A large, illuminated terrarium stood to the right. From underneath a thick floor of moss, foxlike creatures peeked out, then retreated. An elderly orb tortoise pulled a piece of leaf into its shell and curled into a perfect iridescent sphere before rolling into his little water reservoir.
